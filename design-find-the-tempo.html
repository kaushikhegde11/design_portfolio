01  Snapshot

Find the Tempo is a web app. I designed it and built it to solve my own problem. It turns a screenshot of songs into openable streaming links.

  • Role. Solo — UX research, UI design, and front-end build.
  • Timeline · tools. 2026 · Next.js on Netlify, Vertex AI OCR, GA4.
  • Live. findthetempo.netlify.app ↗

02  The problem

Songs reach you scattered across apps — screenshots, Shazams, shared links. To pull them into one usable list is manual work, and the tracks are easy to lose.

This started as my own problem. That personal origin is a strength, not a weakness. I felt the friction before I designed for it.

03  Who it's for

  • Primary user. DJs and everyday music fans who gather tracks from several streaming services and want them in one place.
  • What I know. They collect from many sources and switch apps to listen.
  • What I do not know yet. Which segment actually needs this. The target segment is still under review.

04  Goals & signals

  • User goal. Get scattered tracks openable in one pass, without retyping.
  • Product goal. Validate demand before building more.
  • Success signals. Upload-to-link-open rate, repeat uploads, and survey pricing intent — stated intent, not proven demand.

05  Constraints

  • Technical. Official streaming APIs bring terms, quotas, and OAuth. Every decision below traces back to this.
  • Cost. OCR costs money. GCP credits cover it, so the provider choice stays cost-driven.
  • Time and skill. Solo, with limited time. Ship a small MVP, not a platform.

06  Options considered

  • A — Exact match. Match tracks by catalog ID through official APIs. Precise, but blocked by API terms, quotas, and OAuth.
  • B — Query links. Open a per-service search link. No API, ships fast, lands on the service's own search — but not the exact track.
  • Chosen. B, for a validation MVP. Exact matching waits for proven demand.

07  Decision · OCR

  • Problem. Read track and artist text out of an uploaded image.
  • Options. On-device Tesseract, or a cloud OCR service.
  • Choice. Vertex AI, behind an OCR_PROVIDER switch.
  • Why. The switch swaps OCR backends without touching call sites, so accuracy and cost stay easy to compare. GCP credits cover the bill.
  • Revisit. If accuracy or cost shifts, or a better OCR option appears.

08  Decision · Linking

  • Problem. Open each track on the service the user actually uses.
  • Options. Official-API catalog match, or a per-service query link.
  • Choice. Query-based deep links.
  • Why. This avoids API terms, quotas, and OAuth, and ships fast. The user lands on the service's own search.
  • Revisit. When exact-track matching matters, or a partner API becomes available.

09  The flow

The whole task is one screen, with no account.

  • Upload a screenshot of a tracklist.
  • OCR reads the track and artist for each row.
  • The app cleans the text and builds one query link per service.
  • Open each track in the app you use.

10  Shipped / next

  • Shipped. An MVP that collects links only — query search, no official API — deployed on Netlify with GA4 analytics.
  • Next. Field the DJ and Listener research surveys. Decide the target segment. Rotate the GCP service-account key. Decide on Spotify integration.

Honest status: validating, not proven.

11  Reflection

  • What worked. Fast to ship, it removes real friction, and the OCR switch keeps the backend flexible.
  • What I would change. Validate demand earlier, before building the MVP.

Strongest criticism, answered: the consumer "playlist mover" space is crowded (Soundiiz, TuneMyMusic), and DJ links cannot load into Serato or rekordbox. So product-market fit is unproven, and the DJ pivot is blocked by locked store APIs (Beatport, Bandcamp) and an incumbent, Lexicon. My response: treat this as a validation project and run the surveys before I build more.
